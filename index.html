The Part Nobody Warns You About

Success was supposed to make you freer. It made you more careful.

Here's the pattern in almost every woman who calls me. She's successful. She's respected. She's earning $200K, $500K, and more. And somewhere on the way up, the game quietly changed on her.

She stopped playing to win and started playing not to lose.

The comfort zone shrinks as the numbers grow. There's more to protect now, so she takes fewer chances.

The five quiet behaviours
  • She over-delivers and under-charges.
  • She keeps her end of agreements the other side already dropped.
  • She discounts because she bought a story about what her clients can afford.
  • She waits to be recognized instead of asking for the number.
  • In other words, she goes for gold stars instead of gold bars.

None of it looks like a crisis. That's exactly why it runs for years.

Then one ordinary morning she sits down with her coffee and thinks, "Is this it?"

You are not stuck because you're missing a strategy. You're stuck because you've been leaking power in places you stopped noticing.

What I Believe

What you tolerate, you teach.

Every rate you don't raise teaches someone what you're worth. Every agreement you honor after they've broken theirs teaches them the terms. Every time you shrink to keep the peace, you're training the room.

The reverse is also true, and it moves faster than you'd expect.

I'm not interested in you having more money for its own sake. I'm interested in what more money buys you: the ability to say no, to walk, to choose, to fund the thing you actually care about. More money is more options. More options is freedom.

About Nazy

I learned this the expensive way.

In my early twenties I owned a home, drove a new Porsche, and was the youngest of my peers to make VP. Multiple six-figure net worth. Self-made by anyone's measure.

Then I got married, and I did what I'd been raised to do. I compromised. I accommodated. I took the back seat in my own life, one reasonable-sounding decision at a time. Caught between cultures and expectations, I gave away the one thing that had made me successful so young: my power.

The money followed it out the door.

After the divorce I had nothing. Not just net worth. Self-worth. I was working full time on Wall Street and still had to borrow from my father to rent an apartment.

It was the best thing that ever happened to me, though it took a few years before I could say that out loud.

Once I could see the leaks, I could close them. Six years later my net worth was seven figures. I did it on Wall Street. I did it as a consultant. I did it in my second language, in rooms that weren't built for me. A mentor once congratulated me on how many glass ceilings I'd broken. I thanked him, and I had to go and look up what a glass ceiling was.

I was brought up in a culture with the expectation of becoming a housewife, but that was never my dream. I studied computing and engineering instead, then robotics. Since then I've walked on fire, jumped off a pole, skydived, scuba-dived, swum with dolphins, and moved to London, knowing nobody, among other things. I mention it because the pattern holds everywhere: the thing stopping you is almost never the thing in front of you.
